# Skill: LinkedIn post for a policy publication

## When to use

When a paper has been scheduled with a launch date and the launch-morning
LinkedIn post is needed. One post per publication, from the EMPN account.

## Inputs required

- `title`
- `authors` — names with their institutes
- `summary` — 2 to 4 sentences
- `figure_1`, `figure_2`, `figure_3` — each with its page reference
- `policy_area`
- `post_url`
- `audience_note` — who this launch is aimed at

If any input is missing, or a figure arrives without a page reference, stop and
name what is missing. Never supply a figure from your own knowledge. Never
estimate, round or infer one.

## Rules

**1. Open on the contradiction, not the announcement.**
The first line states the tension in the file. Never begin with "our new
paper", "we are pleased to share", or any variant.
*Test: the first line makes sense to someone who has never heard of this file.*

**2. Three numbers, in an order that argues.**
Use exactly the three supplied figures, sequenced so they build the case: the
scale of what is needed, then how far short delivery falls, then how little of
it the available money covers. The order is the argument. Do not reorder for
rhythm.
*Test: read the three figures alone — they state a case without the text
around them.*

**3. Institutional voice, authors foregrounded.**
The account speaks, but every author and their institute is named in the body.
*Test: the post credits the people who did the work, not the organisation.*

**4. End on a claim, not a request.**
The last line before the link asserts what the paper puts on the table. Never
"read more", "check it out", "what do you think".
*Test: delete the link and the final line still stands as a statement.*

**5. Attribute anything that is not the paper's own.**
A figure from a minister, a government or a journalist is attributed in the
sentence that carries it. Four extra words.
*Test: a reader can tell, without leaving the post, which claims are the
paper's and which belong to someone else.*

**6. Length: 160 to 190 words.**

## What good looks like

> Every EU government agrees Europe needs more grid. Almost none of them agree
> on who should pay for it.
>
> That gap is why the EU Grids Package is stuck, and why it may not pass this
> year.
>
> The numbers behind the argument: of the 88 GW of cross-border capacity Europe
> needs by 2030, 41 GW is still unaddressed. Closing the wider gap takes roughly
> €1.4 trillion. EU grants cover a fraction of that even after the proposed
> fivefold increase to the Connecting Europe Facility, so the rest lands on
> national consumers. Which is precisely what Member States are fighting about.
>
> In our new paper, Mediha Inan and Janek Steitz (Dezernat Zukunft) and Asker
> Voldsgaard (Center for Makrofinans) sort the 27 into four coalitions with
> genuinely different material interests, then set out five compromises that
> would give each of them something.
>
> The Council has agreed its position. Parliament is finalising its own.
> Trilogue negotiators will then need bridging text that nobody has written yet.
>
> That is what this paper puts on the table.

Why this works: it opens on the contradiction rather than the publication; the
three figures run in the order that makes the argument; the authors are named
with their institutes; and it closes on a claim, not a request.

## Never

- Invent, round, estimate or infer a figure
- Use any number that did not arrive in the inputs
- Lead with the publication rather than the argument
- Describe another think tank as a competitor or rival
- More than two hashtags
- "Thrilled", "excited", "delighted", "proud to share"
- Close on a question to farm comments

## Output format

Plain text. No markdown. No preamble, no alternatives, no commentary.
Return only the post.
